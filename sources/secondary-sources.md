# Secondary Histories and Research Guides

Primary sources tell us what people wrote, built and operated at the time. Secondary scholarship helps explain institutions, causal relationships, forgotten participants, standards politics and the danger of retrospective myths.

This bibliography is intentionally multidisciplinary: technical history, business history, infrastructure studies, standards history and participant memoir all belong.

## Core books

### Janet Abbate — *Inventing the Internet*
MIT Press, 1999 (paperback edition 2000).

Publisher: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262511155/inventing-the-internet/

Why it matters:
- packet switching as a contested, socially shaped technology;
- ARPANET and internetworking;
- international influences;
- standards and institutional choices;
- avoids a purely heroic-inventor chronology.

Use it as interpretation, then follow its references back to primary sources.

### Katie Hafner and Matthew Lyon — *Where Wizards Stay Up Late: The Origins of the Internet*
1996.

Why it matters:
- readable narrative of ARPANET people and BBN engineering;
- useful names/anecdotes/research leads;
- particularly valuable for the human side of the IMP project.

Cross-check memorable anecdotes against contemporary records.

### Andrew L. Russell — *Open Standards and the Digital Age: History, Ideology, and Networks*
Cambridge University Press, 2014.

Why it matters:
- standards institutions;
- “openness” as a historical and political concept;
- OSI vs Internet standardization;
- links network architecture to institutions and ideology.

### Martin Campbell-Kelly and Daniel D. Garcia-Swartz — histories of software/network industries
Use for commercial context where networking intersects software industries and online services. Bibliographic expansion needed.

## James Pelkey — *The History of Computer Communications*

Online: https://historyofcomputercommunications.info/

One of the most directly relevant resources for this repository.

Strengths:
- based on more than 80 interviews;
- extensive treatment of modem, data communication, LAN and networking companies;
- business/entrepreneurial history often omitted from protocol-centered accounts;
- preserves people and firms that are otherwise difficult to recover.

Cautions:
- sections may contain chronology/model claims requiring primary-source verification;
- the Bell 101/Bell 103 dating problem already demonstrates why even an excellent secondary source should not end the investigation.

## Internet Society participant histories

### *A Brief History of the Internet*
https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

Authors include Barry Leiner, Vint Cerf, David Clark, Robert Kahn, Leonard Kleinrock, Daniel Lynch, Jon Postel, Larry Roberts and Stephen Wolff.

Value:
- participant synthesis;
- internetworking program;
- TCP/IP;
- DNS;
- institutional evolution;
- commercialization.

Caution: participant history is neither neutral nor worthless. Treat it as informed testimony and compare with archives/scholarship.

### Vint Cerf — *History of the Internet & Related Networks*
https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet-related-networks/

Especially useful because it explicitly covers networks outside ARPANET, including CSNET and BITNET.

## Telecom history

Computer-network history needs scholarship on the telephone/telegraph system itself:

- Bell System leased/private lines;
- modem regulation and Carterfone-era terminal attachment;
- common-carrier regulation;
- digital carrier/T1;
- international PTT structures;
- submarine cable/satellite data service;
- X.25 public data networks;
- ISDN/Frame Relay/ATM.

A packet network's “link” was often an expensive regulated telecommunications product. Without telecom history, the diagram loses its economic substrate.

## Historiographical traps

### “The Internet was invented in 1969”
Conflates ARPANET deployment with later internetworking/TCP/IP evolution.

### “ARPANET was built to survive nuclear war”
Conflates Baran's survivable communications research with the direct rationale and funding history of ARPANET.

### “TCP/IP beat OSI because it was technically superior”
Erases implementation timing, procurement, institutional dynamics, installed bases, politics and standards process.

### “Ethernet was always RJ-45 star cabling”
Erases experimental Ethernet, thick coax, vampire taps, AUI, thin coax and hubs.

### “Before the Internet, computers were not networked”
Erases SAGE, SABRE, public data networks, proprietary networks, BITNET, X.25, BBS networks and many others.

### “The Web is the Internet”
Erases decades of networking and many non-Web Internet services.

### “A protocol specification equals a deployed protocol”
Erases implementation/version/operational history.

## Research principle

Secondary history should help formulate better questions, not replace the archive.

When a secondary source says something memorable, ask:

1. What source is it based on?
2. Is the date proposal, publication, shipment or deployment?
3. Is a modern term being projected backward?
4. Are competing systems omitted because they eventually lost?
5. Can the hardware/software/document be identified precisely?

That conversion—from narrative claim back into inspectable evidence—is the core method of this repository.

# Master Timeline: Computer Networking Archaeology

This timeline is a **chronological spine**, not a substitute for technical articles. Dates can mean proposal, demonstration, deployment, standardization or retirement; the event text should say which.

Where a year is disputed or refers to multiple milestones, the timeline deliberately says so.

## 1940s

### 1949 — modem-like digital data transmission work
The Computer History Museum dates modem development for transmitting radar signals by Jack Harrington's group at the Air Force Cambridge Research Center to **1949**. The technology modulated digital data so it could travel over voice-oriented telephone infrastructure.

Source: https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/networking-the-web/

## 1950s

### 1950–1956 — SAGE development era
Whirlwind/Cape Cod work and the Semi-Automatic Ground Environment (SAGE) program drove large-scale real-time computing, remote radar-data transmission and interactive operator-console technology.

### 1953 — modem technology adapted for SAGE, according to CHM timeline
Computer History Museum says modem technology was adapted to computers in 1953 for SAGE.

Source: https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/networking-the-web/

### 1958 — Bell commercialization of data communications / modem chronology requires careful model-level study
Computer History Museum says Bell Telephone commercialized modem technology in **1958**. James Pelkey's *History of Computer Communications* and later references use model names/dates that need reconciliation against primary AT&T documentation. Do not flatten Bell 101/Bell 103 chronology until the original announcements/manuals are checked.

Sources:
- https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/networking-the-web/
- https://historyofcomputercommunications.info/

### 1958 — T1/digital-carrier development milestone
Computer History Museum places development of the North American T1 digital transmission standard in this period as telephone companies digitized internal trunk transmission.

Source: https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/networking-the-web/

## 1960s

### 1960 — SAGE operational era / large-scale computer communications
SAGE connected hardened computing sites with remote sensor/data sources and operator stations. It is best treated as an essential computer-communications precursor, not retroactively as an Internet-like packet network.

Source: https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/computers/

### 1960s — SABRE operational deployment
American Airlines and IBM's SABRE reservation system became operational during the decade and reused engineering lessons from SAGE for real-time remote transaction processing.

Source: https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/computers/

### 1961 — CTSS public demonstration era
MIT's Compatible Time-Sharing System demonstrated interactive multi-user computing; remote-terminal access helped establish the social/technical assumptions from which networking grew.

### 1962–1963 — Licklider's networked-computing vision at ARPA
J. C. R. Licklider led ARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office and argued for interactive, interconnected computing. Exact memo chronology belongs in a dedicated article.

### 1964 — IBM System/360 era and SNA preconditions
The System/360 family reinforced centralized enterprise computing and later networking/controller architectures.

### 1965 — Donald Davies develops packet-switching concept at NPL
NPL's institutional timeline dates Donald Davies and team's packet-switching work to **1965**.

Source: https://www.npl.co.uk/about-us/history/timeline

### mid-1960s — Paul Baran's distributed communications work circulates
RAND reports explored survivable distributed communication and message/block switching ideas. The relationship between Baran, Davies and later packet networking must be told without collapsing independent lines of work into one invention story.

### 1965–1967 — remote computer-to-computer experiments and time-sharing links
Experiments linking computers over communications circuits demonstrated both the possibilities and inefficiencies of using terminal-oriented/circuit-oriented arrangements for computer traffic.

### 1967 — ARPANET design discussions intensify
ARPA researchers moved toward a packet-switched network connecting heterogeneous research computers.

### 1968 — BBN selected to build ARPANET Interface Message Processors
Bolt Beranek and Newman won the contract to construct the IMP packet-switching nodes.

### 7 April 1969 — RFC 1, *Host Software*
Steve Crocker's RFC 1 documented tentative agreements and open questions about IMP software, Host–IMP interaction and host-to-host software before the network was fully operational.

Source: https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc1/

### 1969 — early RFC sequence defines a working-document culture
RFCs 1–10 include host software, documentation conventions, schedules, DEL, conversation notes, Host–IMP interface and ARPA Network functional specifications.

Source: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc-index/

### September 1969 — first ARPANET IMP installed at UCLA
The first switching node was installed at UCLA. Hardware and host-interface details belong in the ARPANET article.

### October 1969 — UCLA–SRI host communication / famous first login attempt
The first attempted host login between UCLA and SRI failed after the letters `L` and `O` were transmitted; later communication succeeded. Preserve the distinction between the famous anecdote and the broader sequence of link/IMP/host bring-up events.

### late 1969 — four-node ARPANET
UCLA, SRI, UCSB and University of Utah formed the initial four-node network.

## 1970s

### 1970 — NPL Data Communications Network operational milestone
NPL's timeline says its packet-switched data communications network was developed by 1970 and served roughly 200 users.

Source: https://www.npl.co.uk/about-us/history/timeline

### 1970–1971 — ARPANET expands beyond initial four sites
More hosts and IMPs joined; operational problems began to drive protocol and routing revisions.

### June 1971 — ALOHAnet begins inter-island packet-radio service
University of Hawaiʻi records June 1971 as the beginning of ALOHA packet-radio data networking between islands.

Source: https://www.eng.hawaii.edu/about/history/alohanet/

### 1971–1972 — network email emerges on ARPANET
Ray Tomlinson's network mail experiments and subsequent mail software helped turn email into a major network workload. Exact dates/program names need primary-source treatment.

### 1972 — public ARPANET demonstration at ICCC
The International Conference on Computer Communications demonstrated the growing ARPANET and catalyzed broader interest in packet networking.

### 1972 — CYCLADES project launched at IRIA
Louis Pouzin led the French CYCLADES project; its CIGALE packet-switching subnet used Mitra 15 minicomputers.

Source: https://www.inria.fr/en/arpanet-internet-france-some-milestones

### 1973 — CYCLADES datagram network demonstration
Inria dates the official CYCLADES demonstration to 1973 and emphasizes its datagram/end-system reliability ideas.

Source: https://www.inria.fr/en/louis-pouzin-et-internet

### 1973 — Ethernet conceived at Xerox PARC
Robert Metcalfe circulated the Alto Ethernet memo in 1973; Ethernet adapted shared-medium/random-access ideas from ALOHAnet to coaxial cable.

Sources:
- https://www.comsoc.org/node/19561
- https://spectrum.ieee.org/xerox-parc/ethernet-history

### 1973 — DARPA Internetting research program
Internet Society participant histories date the Internetting project to 1973, aimed at interconnecting independently designed packet networks.

Source: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet-related-networks/

### May 1974 — Cerf and Kahn publish *A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication*
The paper formalized a transmission-control architecture for internetworking. Later TCP/IP split and version history must be treated separately.

### mid-1970s — Telenet and commercial packet networking expand
Commercial packet-switched data networks showed that packet networking could be sold as a service, not merely operated as a research experiment.

### 1976 — CCITT adopts X.25
X.25 established a virtual-circuit-oriented packet communication standard and became foundational to many public data networks.

Source: https://www.comsoc.org/node/19621

### late 1970s — PRNET and SATNET internetworking experiments
DARPA experiments linked heterogeneous packet radio, satellite and ARPANET environments, helping test internetworking protocols under different media assumptions.

### 1978 — *Completion Report: ARPA Network Development*
Frank Heart, Alex McKenzie, John McQuillan and Dave Walden documented the ARPANET development program.

Archive record: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Arpanet_Completion_Report.pdf

### 1978–1979 — TCP architecture split into TCP and IP
The originally monolithic transmission-control design evolved toward separate internetwork and reliable transport layers. Exact IEN/RFC chronology belongs in a dedicated protocol genealogy.

### 1979 — Usenet begins
Tom Truscott and Jim Ellis at Duke, with Steve Bellovin at UNC involvement in early work, built a distributed news system propagated initially using UUCP/dial-up links. Exact first-node chronology needs dedicated sourcing.

## 1980s

### 1980–1981 — CSNET and BITNET begin
Internet Society's related-network history dates CSNET and BITNET to 1980–81. CSNET used PhoneNet/MMDF for telephone mail relaying and pioneered TCP/IP over X.25; BITNET used IBM RSCS/NJE lineage and leased lines.

Source: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet-related-networks/

### April 1981 — BBN Report 4799, *A History of the ARPANET: The First Decade*
A 183-page BBN technical history preserved project objectives, engineering and operational lessons.

Archive: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:A_History_of_the_ARPANET,_The_First_Decade,_BBN_Report_4799,_April_1981.pdf

### 1982 — SMTP standardized in early form
RFC 821 and related documents moved Internet mail toward the SMTP architecture.

### 1 January 1983 — ARPANET NCP-to-TCP/IP transition
The coordinated transition is a standard milestone for TCP/IP deployment. It should be understood as a protocol migration across an existing network, not a single instant in which every notion of “Internet” suddenly appeared.

### 1983 — Domain Name System design period
Paul Mockapetris' DNS work addressed the scaling limits of centralized host tables.

### 1984 — JANET begins service
The UK academic network initially relied heavily on the UK Colored Book protocol suite; its later TCP/IP transition illustrates that global academic networking did not begin uniformly with TCP/IP.

### 1984 — FidoNet emerges
BBS systems begin exchanging mail/messages automatically, building a large store-and-forward hobbyist network outside the Internet proper.

### 1984 — DNS deployment expands
RFC 882/883 era implementations precede the later RFC 1034/1035 foundation.

### 1985 — NSF planning for national supercomputer networking
NSF planning laid the groundwork for NSFNET.

Source: https://nsf.net/timeline

### 1985 — NSF chooses TCP/IP direction for NSFNET
The NSFNET historical project records Dennis Jennings' December 1985 decision to use TCP/IP.

Source: https://nsf.net/timeline

### 1986 — NSFNET 56 kbit/s backbone operational
The initial backbone linked supercomputer centers using Fuzzball routers on DEC PDP-11 systems and 56 kbit/s circuits.

Sources:
- https://nsf.net/timeline
- https://nsf.net/projects/backbone

### 1986 — NSF.net registered
NSFNET historical records date registration of `nsf.net` to 5 November 1986.

Source: https://nsf.net/timeline

### 1987 — NSF awards expanded backbone work to Merit with IBM and MCI
The public-private partnership would build the T1/T3 NSFNET backbone generations.

Sources:
- https://nsf.net/timeline
- https://www.merit.edu/research/projects/the-nsfnet-backbone-service/

### 1988 — T1 NSFNET backbone enters service
Merit/IBM/MCI's expanded backbone operated at 1.544 Mbit/s; NSFNET project records July 1988 service.

Source: https://nsf.net/projects/backbone

### 1988 — Internet congestion-control crisis and Van Jacobson algorithms
Congestion collapse episodes drove major TCP congestion-control changes. Preserve exact code/RFC chronology in a TCP article.

### late 1980s — commercial Internet providers and multiprotocol networking grow
The Internet coexisted with OSI, X.25, SNA, DECnet, XNS/IPX and numerous other stacks; routers often had to support multiple protocol families.

### 1989 — NSFNET exceeds 500 connected networks
NSFNET historical project records this growth milestone.

Source: https://nsf.net/achievements

### 1989 — early international NSFNET links broaden
Connections to research networks in Canada, Europe, Australia, Japan and elsewhere helped transform “the Internet” into a genuinely international infrastructure.

## 1990s

### 1990 — ARPANET decommissioned
ARPANET's retirement did not end the Internet; by then its architectural descendants and interconnected networks had outgrown it.

### 1990 — NSFNET traffic scale reaches billions of packets per month
The NSFNET project records 10 billion packets/month as a 1990 milestone.

Source: https://nsf.net/achievements

### 1990–1991 — commercial-network disputes intensify
ANS and ANS CO+RE arrangements became focal points in debates over public backbone infrastructure, acceptable use and commercial traffic.

Source: https://nsf.net/about

### 1991 — NSFNET T3 era
NSFNET historical records place the backbone at 45 Mbit/s by 1991; RS/6000 systems replaced the multi-RT-node architecture.

Source: https://nsf.net/projects/backbone

### 1991 — World Wide Web begins spreading outside CERN
The Web enters networking history as an application and traffic driver, not as the origin of the Internet.

### 1992 — NSFNET connects internationally to dozens of countries
The NSFNET project reports connections to 38 countries by 1992.

Source: https://nsf.net/achievements

### 1993 — NCSA Mosaic accelerates Web adoption
Graphical Web access changes user demand, traffic patterns and commercial interest in Internet connectivity.

### 1993–1994 — BGP-4 and CIDR address scaling pressures
Interdomain routing and address aggregation evolve to manage rapid Internet growth.

### 1994 — NSFNET carries 60.6 billion packets in June
NSFNET's project archive records this traffic milestone.

Source: https://nsf.net/achievements

### 1994 — vBNS planning/contract period
NSF transitions high-end research networking support toward a new model while commercial providers take over general backbone traffic.

### 30 April 1995 — NSFNET Backbone Service ends
The NSFNET historical project and Merit record the retirement of the NSFNET backbone service, with traffic moving to commercial providers and new interconnection arrangements.

Sources:
- https://nsf.net/timeline
- https://www.merit.edu/research/projects/the-nsfnet-backbone-service/

### 24 October 1995 — Federal Networking Council Internet definition
The FNC adopted a formal definition centered on globally unique IP addressing and TCP/IP or compatible protocol support.

Source: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

## After 1995

The archive continues where necessary to explain the disappearance or transformation of older infrastructure:

- dial-up modem peak and decline;
- Ethernet switching/full duplex;
- 100 Mbit/s → gigabit LAN transition;
- commercial backbone/router scaling;
- broadband access;
- IPv6;
- NAT;
- MPLS;
- Wi-Fi;
- data-center networking;
- software-defined networking.

But the main archaeological emphasis remains on the period in which networking concepts and hardware were still visibly diverse enough that today's abstractions had not yet hidden their ancestry.

## Timeline discipline

Never add a date here merely because it appears in a modern unsourced timeline. A future machine-readable version should tag each event as:

`proposal | prototype | test | demonstration | deployment | standard | publication | commercial-launch | retirement`

and link it to at least one source-ledger record.
